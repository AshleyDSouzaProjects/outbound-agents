# 10 — Pulak Prasad / Nalanda

Source: *What I Learned About Investing from Darwin*, Nalanda's public investment approach,
and this repo's own return decomposition (`../../` — the Nalanda analysis in session history).

---

## The core asymmetry: Type I vs Type II errors

Prasad's central insight, borrowed from biology. Two ways to be wrong:

- **Type I** — buying a bad business. Costs real, often permanent, capital.
- **Type II** — passing on a good business. Costs an opportunity you never had.

These are **not symmetric**, but almost every investor treats them as if they are, because
missing a multibagger feels painful and visible while avoided disasters are invisible.

Prasad deliberately accepts **many** Type II errors to avoid Type I errors. He rejects the
overwhelming majority of companies quickly and without agonising, accepting that some will go
on to do brilliantly.

> **System implication:** rejection-first funnel. Cheap brutal filters on all 500 before any
> expensive analysis. When genuinely torn, reject. Log the reason; revisit annually.

## Rejection criteria (the first gate)

Reject fast and without sentiment on:

- **Governance** — promoter pledging, related-party transactions of meaningful size,
  auditor resignations or unexplained changes, weak board independence.
- **Capital allocation** — serial dilution, value-destroying acquisitions, unexplained
  diversification into unrelated businesses.
- **Business quality** — persistently low or volatile ROCE, no pricing power, commodity
  economics with no cost advantage.
- **Leverage** — meaningful debt in a cyclical business. Debt converts a survivable mistake
  into a permanent one.

Note what is *absent*: valuation is not a first-pass rejection criterion. A wonderful business
at a rich price stays on the watchlist. A bad business at any price is rejected permanently.

## Buy high quality at a fair price

Not "cheap." **Fair.** Prasad explicitly does not hunt for statistical bargains — the deep-value
screen is a good way to accumulate Type I errors. Quality first, then price discipline.

The quality bar is primarily **high and persistent ROCE**, because it is the hardest metric to
fake over long periods and the most direct evidence of a moat.

## "Don't be lazy — be very lazy"

Extreme low turnover. Nalanda describes itself as a **permanent owner**: as long as the
business retains its competitive position, they have committed to never selling.

The insight is that most of the return comes from a small number of positions held for a very
long time, and every sale risks terminating a compounding arc early. Our own decomposition
found Page Industries returned ~51x from Nalanda's entry — achievable *only* by not selling at
5x, or 10x, or 20x.

> **System implication:** "no action" is the expected output. The system must not be scored on
> activity, and must never manufacture ideas to appear productive.

## Historical financials over forecasts

Prasad is sceptical of DCF and of forecasting generally. The emphasis is on what a business has
*demonstrably done* over 10–15 years, not what a model says it might do.

> **System implication:** valuation uses EPV and reverse-DCF (what does the price imply?)
> rather than forward-DCF (what should the price be?). See `12-other-masters.md` on
> Greenwald and Mauboussin.

## Concentration

~8–10% stakes, a portfolio of roughly 25–40 names since inception, and today reportedly ~63%
in two positions. Concentration amplifies selection quality — **in both directions.** It is a
multiplier on skill, and equally a multiplier on error.

## What the record does NOT support

Our own analysis of Nalanda's history (see session history and `PLAN.md` §6) found:

- **Market timing contributed roughly nothing.** Their celebrated Oct-2008 "crash" purchase of
  Page Industries at ₹450 was *25% more expensive* than simply buying the March-2007 IPO at ₹360.
  A naive monthly SIP across 2005–2010 would have roughly matched their actual deployment.
- **They did not sit in cash.** They closed a $400M fund in May 2007 and deployed into a market
  near its top, then across 2007–2012. The GFC arriving 12 months after fund close was *vintage
  luck*, not foresight.
- **~38% of the Page return came from multiple re-rating** (P/E ~14x → ~65x), not earnings.
  That lever is spent and now works against the strategy.

> **System implication — important:** do not encode market timing. Do not tune the system on
> 2010–2020 data expecting a repeat of the quality re-rating. Encode the *rejection discipline*
> and the *holding period*, which are the parts of the record that actually hold up.

## Encoded rules

1. Reject first, reject cheaply, reject often; log every reason.
2. Never reject on valuation alone — watchlist instead.
3. Never accept low or volatile ROCE, whatever the story.
4. Never accept meaningful leverage in a cyclical.
5. Governance failure is an absolute veto, not a discount to be priced in.
6. Default action is nothing.
7. Sell only on structural thesis break, never on price.
